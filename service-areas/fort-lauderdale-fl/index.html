Where the yard stopped being a lawn a long time ago

Fort Lauderdale properties tend to be smaller and closer together than the ones further west, and a great many of them sit on or near the waterway network the city is built around. Outside space is at a premium in the literal sense: there is not much of it, and it is used constantly.

So the brief here is rarely "somewhere to put a table". It is closer to adding a room — a defined outdoor space with a floor, edges, shade and a relationship to the doors it opens off. That is a design problem worth taking seriously, because on a lot this size there is nowhere for a mistake to hide.

Two things shape how it goes, and both are settled before any material is chosen. The first is how the work physically gets onto the site, which on many of these lots is genuinely difficult. The second is what the coastal air does to whatever is installed. Neither is glamorous. Both decide more about the result than the choice between one pale stone and another.

The first constraint

How the work gets in

On a large lot this is a footnote. Here it is the thing that shapes the method, the sequence and sometimes the design.

Often there is no rear route

Where a property backs onto a canal, a neighbour or a wall, everything in and out goes past the side of the house. That single fact changes how material is delivered, how spoil leaves and how long groundwork takes.

Side gates set the equipment

The width of the tightest pinch point decides what machinery can reach the back. Below a certain width the work becomes hand-barrow and small-plant work, which is entirely doable and needs to be priced and programmed as what it is.

Nowhere to stack material

With no space to store a full delivery, material arrives in stages matched to the sequence. That takes coordination rather than luck, and it is the main reason a tight site rewards planning.

The street is part of the site

Narrow frontages, on-street parking and short driveways mean deliveries and equipment interact with the road. What that involves is confirmed for your address rather than assumed, and it is worth settling before a start date is fixed.

Everything finished gets protected

When the route runs past the house, the route is somebody's paving, planting and paintwork. Protection is planned rather than improvised, because damage repaired at the end costs more than covering it at the start.

Neighbours are close

On a compact lot the work happens a few feet from someone else's window. Sensible hours, tidy edges and telling people what is coming are part of doing the job properly.

The second constraint

What coastal air does to a specification

Inland, the things that age an outdoor space are sun, humidity and driving rain. Nearer the water all three still apply and one more joins them: salt-laden air, working continuously on anything metallic.

It shows up in the components, not the slabs. Fixings, brackets, hinges, latches, railings, the hardware on a gate, the fasteners holding a structure down — these are where coastal exposure is felt first and worst. A specification written for an inland property and copied to a waterfront one will usually look identical on paper and behave differently in three years.

Which is why we specify those parts for the exposure rather than by habit, and why it is worth asking any contractor what they are proposing to fix your structure down with. It is an unglamorous question that separates people who have built near the water from people who have not.

Paving is affected more indirectly. Stone and concrete units are not corroded by salt air in the way metal is, but humidity and shade near the water do encourage growth on surfaces, which is a traction question as much as an appearance one. Cleaning the routes people walk on wet matters more here than it does two miles inland.

None of this makes coastal work difficult. It makes it different, and the difference belongs in the specification rather than in a warranty conversation later.

The brief

Outdoor living on a footprint that will not forgive you

Decide what it is for, then measure it

A compact yard can comfortably hold one primary use and one secondary one. Trying to fit dining, lounging, a fire feature, a grill run and a lawn into the same space produces a set of areas that are each slightly too small to use. Choosing the primary use is the single most valuable decision on a small site.

Clearances are not optional

A dining table needs room for chairs to pull out behind people who are seated, and a walkway past a lounge setting needs to be wide enough to use with a drink in your hand. Those dimensions do not shrink because the yard did. Working from real clearances is how a small space ends up feeling generous rather than tight — the same discipline the patios page goes into in detail.

Use the vertical

Where floor area is scarce, screening, planting and overhead cover do work that paving cannot. A structure over part of the space adds usable hours without adding a square foot, and a screen in the right place buys privacy from a window that overlooks you.

The doors matter more than usual

When the outdoor room is small and adjacent, the threshold between inside and out becomes the main event. Level changes, widths and where the glass sits all affect whether the two spaces read as one — which is why window and door replacement often belongs in the same conversation on these properties.

Around water

Pool areas when the pool takes most of the yard

A pool on a compact lot leaves a surround rather than a deck — a band of paving a few feet wide on two or three sides, and one wider zone where people actually sit. Getting that geometry right is most of the job.

Width is the decision. A surround narrow enough to walk single file is fine on the far side and hopeless on the side people use. Concentrating the available space into one usable zone, rather than distributing it evenly around the water, is almost always the better answer.

The wet routes are short and constant. Steps out of the pool, the path to the door, the towel corner — on a small deck these overlap, so the finish underfoot gets specified for wet use rather than assumed. No surface is slip-proof; what varies is how much traction a finish keeps when it is wet, which the pool decks page sets out properly.

The water still has to leave outward. Even a narrow surround is graded away from the pool, which on a lot with boundaries close on both sides means the fall and any drainage run are designed rather than assumed.

The pool itself is not our trade. Everything from the coping inward — the vessel, its finish, the tile at the waterline, the pipework and the plant — is a licensed pool contractor's responsibility, and it should stay there. We build the surround and meet them at the coping, which on a site with one way in is worth agreeing before either trade turns up.

Where an enclosure is part of the project, note that a fence is not automatically a pool barrier — what is required is confirmed for your property with the authority that reviews it, never claimed in advance from a product description.

The floor

Hardscaping the whole footprint

On these lots the paving is not a feature within the yard. It is the yard, so it is worth building like the structure it is.

The buried half decides the visible half

How deep the ground was taken out, how well it was compacted, and what is holding the perimeter together are what keep a surface sitting flat years later. None of that changes because the area is small — but the tolerances feel tighter, because a settlement of half an inch is obvious in a space you can take in at a glance. The method is set out on the paver installation page.

Setting out is visible everywhere

In a small space, every cut is near something. Where the pattern starts, which edge gets the full units and how the paving meets a wall or a threshold are decisions that get looked at daily, so they are made deliberately rather than left to where the run happens to end.

Level changes earn their place

A single step can define a zone more effectively than a change of material and without eating floor area. It also has to be safe, consistent and obvious, which is a detailing question rather than a styling one.

Complete transformation

The argument for doing it in one go

Everywhere else, phasing a project over several years is a reasonable way to manage cost. On a constrained coastal site the arithmetic shifts, and it is worth understanding why before deciding.

The expensive part of working here is not the paving. It is the access — getting equipment and material through a narrow route, protecting everything along the way, staging deliveries because there is nowhere to stack, and removing spoil by the same path. That cost is paid in full every time a project starts, and it barely changes whether you are doing one element or four.

Do the surround, the terrace, the screening and the structure as one project and that access is opened once. Do them in four separate years and it is opened four times, each time over paving that is by then finished and has to be protected or repaired.

There is a second reason. On a small site the elements physically depend on each other — a structure needs footings under paving that has not been laid yet, a drainage run has to pass beneath a surface, a threshold height governs the whole floor. Sequencing all of that once is straightforward; retrofitting it later means opening the thing you just built.

None of which means everything has to happen at once. It means the plan should cover the whole yard even if the build is staged, and that anything buried belongs in the first stage. Complete outdoor remodeling explains how the sequence runs.

Design for the climate

Sun, storms and salt on a small site

Shade is scarce and vital

A small yard usually has fewer mature trees and more reflected heat off walls. Built cover over the primary zone is what makes the space usable in the afternoon.

Reflected light and heat

Pale walls close on two or three sides bounce light and warmth back into the space. It affects material choice and where you would want to sit far more than it does on an open lot.

Storms and loose objects

Close neighbours mean anything not fixed down is a shared problem. What is anchored and what gets brought in is worth deciding during design.

Growth in humid shade

Shaded, damp corners near the water grow things on paving. Cleaning the wet routes is a traction measure, not housekeeping.

Rain with nowhere to run

Heavy summer rain on a fully paved footprint has to be given a route out. That is designed in, and it is why the extent of paving is a drainage decision as well as a lifestyle one.

Hardware takes the beating

Everything that hinges, latches, bolts or slides is on the front line near the water — a specification issue rather than a maintenance one.

How it runs

Access first, then everything else

  1. Measure the route in

    Before the yard, the way to it: gate widths, the tightest pinch point, overhead clearance, the surface the route crosses and where a delivery can stop. This determines method and programme, so it comes first.

  2. Assess the space itself

    Existing surfaces, levels, where water currently goes, what overlooks the yard, how the doors work and what the neighbours' boundaries are doing. On a small lot all of it is relevant.

  3. Set the primary use

    What the space is mainly for, worked out from real clearances rather than from a layout that looks balanced on paper. This is where the design gets decided.

  4. Specify for the exposure

    Materials chosen for the space, and fixings, hardware and finishes chosen for coastal air. Any marine work identified as a separate trade at this point.

  5. Confirm requirements and logistics

    What the municipality requires at your address, plus the practical side — deliveries, equipment, the street, and the hours the work will run.

  6. Build, protect, hand over

    Groundwork and drainage, buried services, footings, surfaces, then screening and finishing — with the access route protected throughout, cleaned down at the end, and the finished space walked with you.

Common questions

Questions from Fort Lauderdale homeowners

There is no way to get machinery into my back yard. Can the work still be done?

Usually, and it is one of the first things we establish rather than one of the last. Where the only route is through a side gate, a garage or the house itself, material moves by hand or on small equipment, spoil leaves the same way, and the sequence changes to suit. What it affects is programme and method, and occasionally whether a particular material in a particular size is sensible. Telling us at the first call is worth more than any other piece of information about the property, because a proposal written for a site with rear access and delivered to a site without it is wrong from the first day.

Does being near the water change the materials you would use?

It changes the metal more than the stone. Salt-laden air works continuously on fixings, hardware, brackets and coatings, so those get specified with the exposure in mind rather than chosen by habit — and it matters for gates, structures, railings and anything with a moving part. Paving materials are affected less directly, though the way a surface is cleaned and what grows on it in humid shade both differ near the water. None of this is exotic; it is the difference between a specification written for a coastal property and one copied from an inland job.

Do you work on docks, seawalls or anything at the waterline?

No. Docks, seawalls, boat lifts and any structure in or retaining the water are a marine trade with its own licensing, engineering and permitting, and that is where they belong. We build the outdoor space on the land side and stop at the point where marine work begins. On a property where both are happening, the sensible thing is to say so early so the two programmes are planned around each other rather than colliding on a site with one access route.

My yard is small. Is a full outdoor living project worth it?

Frequently it is more worth it than on a large lot, because on a compact site every square foot is in use and nothing is decorative. The risk is different too: on a big yard a mediocre layout is forgiving, while on a small one a badly placed step or a walkway six inches too narrow is felt every day. What a small site rewards is precision — accurate setting out, clean edges, and a plan that decides what the space is for before it decides what it is made of.

Where does water go when there is nowhere for it to go?

That is the honest difficulty on a tight lot, and it is a design question rather than a drainage-product question. Falls have to be worked out across a small area with neighbouring property close on both sides, so the answer is often a combination of shallow grades, a channel or run, and being realistic about how much of the footprint can be paved. Where the existing yard already holds water after a storm, that is dealt with during the groundwork — laying a new surface over it moves the problem rather than solving it.

Do you handle permitting in Fort Lauderdale?

Permitting is part of what we take on, and what your address actually requires is established during the project rather than assumed from the last job. The municipality and the county set those requirements and neighbouring cities do not share them, which is why no rule is quoted on this page. On a constrained coastal site there is a second layer worth raising early: how equipment and deliveries reach the property, which can involve the street as well as the lot, and which is worth establishing before a start date is agreed.

Tell us how we would get in

Gate width, the route past the house, and what the yard has to do. A free on-site visit in Fort Lauderdale, and a proposal written for the site you actually have.
