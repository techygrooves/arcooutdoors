Most of these questions get answered badly online

Search for almost any outdoor remodeling question and you will find the same article fifteen times: a list of benefits, a cost range that spans a factor of four, a statistic with no source attached, and a conclusion that happens to recommend whatever the site sells.

These guides are written the other way round. Each one is organised around a decision a homeowner actually has to make, gives the reasoning on both sides, and says plainly where the answer depends on your property rather than on a general rule. Where a number would normally appear, you will usually find the thing that drives the number instead — which is more use when you are comparing quotes.

There are no prices, no timelines, no return-on-investment figures and no invented statistics anywhere in them, for the reason set out in the questions at the foot of this page. Where a topic touches permitting or codes, the article says that requirements vary by jurisdiction and scope, and stops there.

The standard

What you will not find in these articles

Published here for the same reason our project and review standards are: a rule that only exists internally is one that quietly relaxes.

No costs or timelines

Both depend on access, existing conditions and scope. A published range either spans so widely it decides nothing, or is narrow enough to mislead somebody whose property does not match the assumption behind it.

No invented statistics

No percentages, no "studies show", no figures with a year bolted on to look current. If a number is not something we can point at a source for, it is not in the article.

No return-on-investment claims

Curb appeal and enjoyment are real. A percentage of cost recouped at resale is not something a contractor can honestly promise you, so we do not print one.

No codes, permits or laws quoted

Requirements vary by jurisdiction and by scope, and they change. Articles flag where approvals are likely to matter; what applies to your address is confirmed with the reviewing authority as part of the project.

No product ratings or brand claims

Performance figures belong to a specific product in a specific configuration and come from the manufacturer's documentation, not from a page like this one.

No invented author

Published by Arco Outdoors as a company. No stock photograph of a person, no fabricated years of experience, no credentials nobody can verify.

Common questions

About these guides

Who writes these articles?

Arco Outdoors publishes them as a company rather than under an individual byline. We would rather attribute them to the business than invent a named expert with credentials nobody can check, which is a common enough practice on contractor websites to be worth saying out loud. If you want to know who you would actually be dealing with on a project, that is a question for the consultation, not for an author box.

Why are there no prices or timelines in any of these guides?

Because a number published to everyone is a number measured on nobody's property. Cost and duration on outdoor work depend on access, existing conditions, levels, drainage, material choice and how much of the yard is involved, and any figure we printed here would be either so wide as to be useless or specific enough to mislead. What you get instead is the reasoning: what drives the number up, what brings it down, and what to ask about when you compare quotes.

Do these articles tell me what permits I need?

No, and be wary of any that claim to. Requirements vary by jurisdiction and by the scope of the work, and what applies in one municipality may not apply in the next one over. What the articles do is flag where approvals are likely to be part of a project, so you can raise it early rather than discover it late. For your own address, the requirements get confirmed with the authority that reviews the work as part of the project.

Are you going to try to sell me something?

Each article ends with a way to reach us, and that is the extent of it. The body is written to be useful whether or not you ever call, including in the places where the honest answer is that a particular option is not worth it, that a smaller scope is the better idea, or that the work belongs to a trade that is not ours. If a guide only makes sense as an argument for hiring us, it has failed at being a guide.

How often is this updated?

These are evergreen guides rather than news, so they change when the advice changes rather than on a schedule. Each article carries the date it was published and the date it was last revised, and where something is genuinely uncertain the article says so instead of picking a side for the sake of a confident-sounding sentence.

Can you write about something specific?

Yes, and the questions people actually ask are where most of these came from. If you are weighing a decision that is not covered here, call and ask it directly — you will get the answer in the conversation rather than having to wait for an article, and it may well become the next one.

Reading is cheaper than rebuilding

When you have narrowed it down, a free on-site visit will tell you which parts of the general advice actually apply to your property — and which do not.
