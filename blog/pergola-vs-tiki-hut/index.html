Pergola vs Tiki Hut: Choosing Shade

Two structures solving one problem in opposite ways. Four questions settle it, and none of them is about the look.

Published 2 September 2026 Arco Outdoors Shade Structures

The short version

A pergola is an open frame. It breaks up the light, keeps the sky visible and leaves the space feeling like part of the yard. A tiki hut is a roof. It gives you a dense, dark, genuinely cool pocket that feels like a separate place to be.

People usually arrive at this decision talking about style — coastal versus contemporary, tropical versus architectural. That is the least useful way in. The four questions below get to an answer faster, and they occasionally produce a third answer, which is that neither one is what the yard actually needs.

Side by side

What each one actually is

The pergola

Posts, beams and rafters over an open space. Shade comes from the spacing and depth of the members, so it moves through the day and never becomes total. Rain passes straight through unless something is added over or under it.

It reads as architecture rather than as an object in the garden, which makes it the easier of the two to relate to a contemporary house. It also keeps a small yard feeling open, because you can still see sky through it.

The tiki hut

A framed structure with a thatched roof over it. The roof is solid cover, so the shade underneath is deep and constant, and the space genuinely feels cooler than the yard around it.

It reads as a destination — somewhere you go to, rather than a modified part of the terrace. Thatch comes in natural and synthetic families, each trading appearance, upkeep and longevity differently, and which one suits a project is settled at consultation rather than declared in advance.

The decision

Four questions, in order

  1. How much sun do you want stopped?

    If the goal is to take the edge off a bright terrace while keeping it light, a pergola does that and a solid roof will feel gloomy. If the goal is a spot that is properly cool at two in the afternoon, dappled shade will disappoint you and thatch will not. This question alone resolves most cases.

  2. Does it need to work in rain?

    Summer downpours are short and heavy. Under a pergola you go inside; under a thatched roof you generally do not. If the point of the structure is to keep an outdoor evening going regardless of weather, that is an argument for a roof — or for a pergola with a covering system added, which is a different product and price.

  3. What does it have to look like from the house?

    A structure of any size is visible from the main rooms for the rest of the time you own the property. A pergola tends to disappear into a contemporary elevation; a thatched roof asserts itself and either suits the yard's character or fights it. Stand at the window you look out of most and picture both.

  4. What are you willing to look after?

    Both are exterior structures in a demanding climate and both need attention. The specifics differ by material and by what the structure is made of, so ask for the actual upkeep expectation for what is being proposed — and be sceptical of any answer that involves no maintenance at all.

The third answer

Sometimes neither. If the area you want shaded is small, sits against the house, and mainly needs cover for a table, an extended roof overhang or a simple awning may do the job for a fraction of the disruption — and where mature trees already shade the space at the right hours, the honest recommendation can be to build nothing at all and move the furniture.

True of either

What both structures need from the project

Foundations, before paving

Anything with posts needs footings, and footings go in before the surface around them. That makes the structure's position an early decision even when the build itself is a later phase.

A position checked against the sun

Where shade lands at four in the afternoon is not obvious from a plan. Standing in the yard at the hours you actually use it is worth more than any drawing.

Scale related to the house

A structure that ignores the proportions of the elevation behind it looks like an object that arrived separately, whichever type it is.

Approvals, likely

Requirements for structures vary by jurisdiction and by scope. Expect approvals to be part of the project and expect a contractor to confirm what applies at your address rather than assert it in advance.

An honest position on wind

Structural performance figures belong to a specific engineered design or a specific product, from its documentation. Treat a general rating quoted before anyone has designed anything as marketing.

Somewhere for water to go

A roof concentrates rain into a line at its edge. Where that water lands, and what it does to the paving and planting beneath it, is a design question rather than an afterthought.

The detail on how each is built sits on the pergolas and tiki huts service pages, including the parts of the decision — footings, position, approvals — that are the same either way.

Shade also does more for a pool deck than any material specification, which is why this decision keeps turning up inside other people's projects.

Where each one comes up

On the generous, open rear lots in Parkland there is room for a structure to sit away from the house as its own destination, which is where thatch tends to win. In Coral Springs the request is usually cover over the part of a pool deck where adults sit, close to the house and competing with children for space — a pergola's openness suits that better. And in Weston, whichever you prefer, the community's guidelines are worth reading before the design is fixed rather than after.
